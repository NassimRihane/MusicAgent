# Design decisions

This document records every design choice of MusicAgent, with its justification and the evidence behind it. Each entry says what was chosen, why, what supports it, what else was considered, and its known limits. Figures are generated by `make_figures.py` (folder `figures/`); numbers come from the reports in `models/`.

## 1. Research goal and scope

**Goal.** Design and evaluate how a system should **give expressive advice** to a piano learner: given an intended emotion, which advice to give, in what order, and how to phrase it. The recognition components (transcription, chords, emotion model) are standard tools, chosen and evaluated only to serve this goal.

**Consequence: one degree of freedom.** Only the expressive feedback (`expressive_feedback.py`) contains original design. Everything it relies on uses widely used, published tools or textbook methods, so that results can be attributed to the advice design and not to a custom recogniser.

### In scope

- Which playing changes to recommend for an intended emotion, how to rank them, and how to phrase them for a learner.
- Whether the advice would move the performance toward the intention.

### Out of scope, and why

| Excluded | Justification |
|---|---|
| **Robustness to background noise** | The research studies the design of the advice, not robust listening. Recordings are made in controlled conditions (quiet room, one piano), as in most performance-analysis studies. Noise would degrade the input to the advice, not change how the advice is designed. Noise robustness is an engineering problem for deployment, which comes after the design is validated. |
| **Improving recognition accuracy** | Recognition is a means. Standard tools are used as they are, and their accuracy is measured (sections 3–6) so that their errors are known when interpreting the advice, not reduced. |
| **Real-time feedback** | Advice is given after a take, as a teacher does after a student plays a passage. Real-time feedback adds latency constraints unrelated to what the advice should say. |
| **Other instruments** | The emotion data (EMOPIA) and the transcription settings are for piano. |
| **Comparing language models** | The language model only phrases advice already chosen by the expressive feedback. Any model can be substituted (`agent.py --model`). |

### Deferred (planned, not excluded)

- **Score-informed analysis** (aligning the performance with the score), which would make rhythm and note correctness measurable and allow advice per phrase.
- **Evaluation of the advice itself**: first a computational measure ("does the advice move the performance toward the intention?"), then a study with learners or teachers.

## 2. Emotion representation: four quadrants

**Decision.** Four emotion classes, the quadrants of Russell's valence–arousal model: Q1 happy/excited, Q2 tense/angry, Q3 sad, Q4 calm/relaxed.

**Why.** It is the standard representation in music emotion recognition and the labelling of EMOPIA, the public piano dataset used to train the model. The quadrants also match the basic emotions studied in music performance research (Juslin: happiness, anger, sadness, tenderness), so advice can rely on that literature. A learner's words (happy, melancholic, tender…) map to a quadrant (`emotion_model.EMOTION_TO_QUADRANT`).

**Alternatives.** Continuous valence and arousal values: finer, but harder to state as an intention and no piano dataset with performance-level annotations. More discrete emotions (e.g. adding fear): no labelled piano data.

**Limits.** Neighbouring emotions within a quadrant (e.g. calm vs tender) cannot be distinguished.

## 3. Emotion model: Random Forest

**Decision.** A Random Forest (scikit-learn) on ten note-level descriptors, trained on EMOPIA (1,071 clips, 387 songs).

**Why.** It is a well-known model, it is at least as accurate as the other standard classifiers on this task, and it provides feature importances, which the expressive feedback uses to rank advice.

**Evidence** (`figures/emotion_model_comparison`, `models/emotion_rf_report.md`). 5-fold cross-validation grouped by song (no song in both training and test), same folds for every model:

| Model | 4 quadrants | Arousal | Valence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Majority class | 28.8 % ± 2.8 | 52.5 % | 51.8 % |
| Logistic regression | 65.9 % ± 1.8 | 88.7 % | 73.6 % |
| k-NN (k = 15) | 64.7 % ± 3.3 | 89.9 % | 70.9 % |
| SVM (RBF kernel) | 67.2 % ± 2.3 | 89.6 % | 74.6 % |
| **Random Forest** | **67.9 % ± 3.1** | 88.7 % | 75.4 % |

All classic classifiers reach a similar level, so the result does not depend on a lucky choice of model. Errors are mostly between quadrants of the same arousal (`figures/emotion_confusion`).

**Limits.** EMOPIA is pop piano transcribed from YouTube recordings with another transcription system; its pedal-extended notes make some typical values (articulation) differ from our transcriptions. Loudness and mode dominate the model (`figures/emotion_feature_importance`); tempo and articulation contribute little, so they rarely become top advice.

## 4. Expressive descriptors

**Decision.** Ten note-level descriptors: tempo, onset rate, timing variability, articulation, mean and spread of loudness (velocity), mean pitch, pitch range, polyphony, and major/minor tendency.

**Why.** They are the performance cues that music-emotion research links to perceived emotion (Juslin & Laukka, 2003; Gabrielsson & Lindström, 2010). They are computed from notes only, because EMOPIA provides MIDI and not audio, so they are computed identically on the training data and on a learner's transcribed performance.

**Open problem: overall loudness is not measured.** Loudness enters the model as the MIDI velocity that basic-pitch estimates for each note. A test shows that this estimate does not depend on the recording level: the same take of *Clair de lune* turned down by 12 dB and 24 dB keeps a mean velocity of 78, 80 and 81. Velocity therefore reflects dynamics *within* a performance (contrast between notes), not how softly the whole piece is played. Consequence: when the user played a sad version 12.6 dB quieter (`sessions/demo_clair_de_lune/clair_de_lune_sad2.wav`), the model did not hear it and still advised "play softer", which is wrong for that take. The recording level cannot replace it without a fixed setup, because it also depends on the microphone, its gain and its distance. Planned fix: a controlled recording setup (same microphone, gain and position, already assumed in the scope), in which the audio level becomes meaningful, and a way to relate it to the model, which was trained on EMOPIA's MIDI velocities. Until then, advice about overall loudness must be treated as unreliable.

**Limits.** Audio-only cues (timbre, brightness) are left out of the model for the same reason; they are reported separately by `expression_analysis.py`. On a digital piano, a softer touch may only change the volume and not the timbre, which this measure would miss.

## 5. Note transcription and key: basic-pitch and music21

**Decision.** Notes are transcribed by basic-pitch (Spotify, ICASSP 2022) on the whole recording; the key is found by music21's Krumhansl–Schmuckler analysis. In minor keys, the raised 6th and 7th degrees count as in the key.

**Why.** basic-pitch is published, polyphonic, and runs locally on a laptop. music21's key analysis is the standard implementation of the standard algorithm.

**Evidence.** Both are evaluated in their publications; on our recordings, the keys of the demo performances are found correctly (C major and A minor). On the 28-chord real recording, transcription is good enough for music21 to name 95 % of the chords (section 6).

**Limits.** basic-pitch shortens notes as the piano sound decays, which affects chord naming in context and the articulation descriptor.

## 6. Chord recognition: music21 by default

**Decision.** Chords are named by music21 from the transcribed notes. A Random Forest on audio chroma is kept as an alternative (`--chords rf`).

**Why.** On a real piano recording, the most direct evidence available, music21 recognises almost every chord type, while the Random Forest never recognises seventh chords. Chords only feed the correction context, not the expressive advice, so no further effort is justified once a sound default is chosen.

**Evidence** (`figures/chord_eval_comparison`, `figures/chord_eval_by_quality`). MIREX scores computed with mir_eval:

| Test | Recogniser | Root | Major/minor | Sevenths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real piano, 28 isolated chords | **music21** | 93 % | 100 % | **95 %** |
| | Random Forest | 100 % | 100 % | 45 % |
| Rendered piano progressions, 36 pieces | music21 | 52 % | 52 % | 36 % |
| | Random Forest | 92 % | 82 % | 70 % |

The two tests disagree. On the real recording, the Random Forest reduces every seventh chord to a triad and every diminished chord to minor or major: a systematic bias from its synthetic training data, which matters for a teaching application. In the rendered progressions, music21 suffers from notes shortened by transcription. The real recording was chosen as the deciding evidence because it is real audio and because music21's errors there are not systematic.

**Alternatives.** Chroma template matching (removed: no temporal context, no evaluation); a custom neural network (removed: not standard, never trained).

**Limits.** One real recording of isolated chords; music21 is weaker on chords played in context.

## 7. Rhythm: tempo stability, not correctness

**Decision.** The correction feedback reports tempo, tempo stability (variation of librosa's local tempo estimate) and hesitations. It does not judge whether the rhythm is *correct*.

**Why.** Without the score, a correct rhythm cannot be defined: an intentional rubato and an unsteady pulse look the same. Stability is the most that can be measured honestly.

**Evidence.** A beat-grid measure (distance of each note to the nearest half or quarter beat) was tried and dropped: on the steady happy demo performance, arpeggios and dotted rhythms made it score worse than random notes. Tempo stability rises with timing jitter and with real tempo changes on synthetic tests, and separates the steady (0.01) from the rubato (0.10) demo performance.

**Limits.** Noisy on short performances with small random jitter. Score-informed analysis (deferred) will replace it.

## 8. Expressive feedback (the research contribution)

**Decision.** For an intended emotion, `expressive_feedback.py`:

1. predicts the emotion the performance conveys (section 3);
2. computes, for each cue the performer controls (tempo, loudness, dynamic contrast, articulation, timing freedom), the gap to typical EMOPIA performances of the intended emotion (distance to the median, in inter-quartile ranges);
3. ranks gaps by size × the model's importance for that cue and turns the top three (above half an inter-quartile range) into advice;
4. checks the advice by re-running the model with those cues moved to their typical values ("what-if").

Cues fixed by the piece (mode, register, pitch range, polyphony, note density) are reported but never advised: a learner cannot change the key of the piece they play.

**Why.** Each step is simple enough to explain to a learner and to defend. Ranking by the model's own importance gives priority to what changes perception most; the what-if check verifies that the advice points in a useful direction. See `figures/advice_example`.

**Phrasing.** The language model receives the gaps in words ("clearly louder than typical sad performances"), not raw numbers. With raw numbers, the model recited values such as "your loudness is 66.2" to the learner. It is also instructed never to ask the learner to remove something the advice asks for (for example rubato in a sad piece).

**Status.** To be evaluated: the planned computational measure applies the advice to EMOPIA clips the model never saw and checks with a *different* model whether the performance moves toward the target, so that the model does not grade its own advice.

## 9. Language model: local Mistral through Ollama

**Decision.** The coach uses Mistral 7B running locally with Ollama. Azure OpenAI remains available as an option.

**Why.** It is free, keeps recordings private, and needs no cloud credits. It only phrases advice that the expressive feedback has already chosen, so its choice does not affect what is advised (section 1).

## 10. Evaluation methodology

- Emotion model: cross-validation grouped by song, compared with standard classifiers on the same folds, mean ± standard deviation over folds.
- Chord recognition: standard MIREX metrics computed with mir_eval, on rendered progressions (exact labels, realistic sound) and on a real recording of a fixed 28-chord protocol (`protocols/chord_recording.md`).
- Every component that is not the research contribution is either a published tool, evaluated in its publication, or evaluated here with a standard metric.
- Tests: 170+ automated tests check each component. They need no microphone, no downloaded data and no network.
